Footwear Fields  ·  report section 4.3, option 2

The harmonic coordinate field

Instead of asking “which point of the foot is nearest?”, it asks “if the foot’s surface labels diffused outward into the surrounding space until they settled, what label would this point hold?” The answer is a coordinate chart that cannot fold, because of a theorem rather than because of tuning.

01

What the chart is for

ΦF takes a point x anywhere in space and returns (u, v, r): where on the foot it sits, and how far outside the foot it lies. Everything downstream is defined on that flat (u, v) sheet — the coverage map C(u,v), the inner clearance o_in(u,v), the material thickness o_out(u,v).

So the chart is load-bearing in the strict sense. If two different points get the same (u, v, r), the material field is asked to be two things at once. If a large volume of shoe collapses onto a thumbnail of skin, the sheet has no resolution left to describe it. Both of those happen with the obvious construction.

02

Where nearest-point breaks

The naive chart shoots the shortest ray from x to the foot surface. That ray is what defines (u, v), and its length is r. Two things go wrong, and they go wrong in opposite directions.

Failure one: crossing, in the concavities

In the arch — and the ankle notch, and the gaps between toes — the surface curves away on both sides. Rays from the two walls run toward each other and meet. A point sitting on that meeting ridge has two nearest points that are far apart along the foot, so it has two legitimate and completely different (u, v) values. Step across the ridge and the coordinate jumps discontinuously from one wall of the arch to the other.

Failure two: collapse, beyond the convexities

The heel is convex, so its rays diverge. A high-heel block, a platform, a thick midsole — all of that volume sits in the diverging fan, and every bit of it projects onto the same small arc of heel skin. The chart is not ambiguous there, it is starved: the (u, v) sheet has a few pixels in which to describe centimetres of material.

The report puts the boundary precisely: the valid region of the naive chart ends roughly at the medial axis of the foot’s exterior. That red ridge in the left panel is the edge of usable territory, and real shoes have plenty of material past it.

03

What harmonic does instead

Drop the ray entirely. Nothing about the coordinate is defined by pointing at the foot from a distance.

Instead, treat the space around the foot as a conducting solid and let the coordinate equilibrate through it. You paint the coordinate on the two boundaries, and the value everywhere in between is whatever settles when the field stops changing.

The physical picture

Hold the foot surface at temperature 0 and a far enclosing surface at temperature 1, then wait. The temperature field that settles is the r coordinate. Its level sets are nested isotherms — onion layers wrapping the foot. Paint a stripe pattern on the foot instead of a temperature and let that settle, and the stripes you get in the volume are the u isolines.

That equilibrium condition is Laplace’s equation, ∇²f = 0: every point holds the average of its neighbours. Solve it once for r with the foot at 0 and the shell at 1. Solve it again for each surface coordinate, with the foot carrying its own surface parameterization and the outer shell left free. Three scalar solves and you have the chart.

04

Why it cannot fold

This is the part that makes option 2 worth the trouble, and it is not a heuristic.

Harmonic functions obey the maximum principle: a solution of Laplace’s equation attains its largest and smallest values only on the boundary, never at an interior point. Since every interior value is the average of its neighbours, no interior point can be a peak or a pit — that is what averaging forbids.

The consequence for us: between the foot at 0 and the shell at 1, every intermediate value appears exactly once along any path from one boundary to the other. There is no interior bump for a level set to close around, and no interior dip for two of them to pinch together. The onion layers nest, in order, by construction.

Compare the failure directly. Nearest-point distance also increases outward, but its gradient is discontinuous at the medial axis — the ray direction flips as you cross. The harmonic field is smooth there. It does not have a medial axis, because it was never built from rays that could meet.

And the heel block improves for the same reason. The field spreads according to the geometry of the gap rather than the direction of a ray, so a fat region of space gets a fat band of r rather than being crushed onto one patch. It does not make the compression vanish, but it distributes it smoothly instead of concentrating it.

05

What you would actually build

  1. Enclose the fitted foot. Build a far outer surface — an inflated, smoothed hull that comfortably contains the largest shoe you care about, heel block included. This surface is a modelling choice and it shapes the chart, so it needs its own fixed convention exactly like the pose rule in section 4.2.
  2. Mesh the gap. Tetrahedralize the volume between the foot and that surface. This is the shell where all coordinates live.
  3. Solve three times. One Laplace solve for r, Dirichlet 0 on the foot and 1 on the shell. Two more carrying the surface parameterization outward, foot values fixed and the shell left Neumann-free. All three are the same sparse system with different right-hand sides, so one factorization serves all of them.
  4. Query by interpolation. For any x, find its tet and read off (u, v, r) barycentrically. Cheap, and differentiable with respect to x, which is what the decoder needs.
  5. Amortize over shape. The fitted feet live in a low-dimensional β space, so solve on a small basis of shapes and interpolate the fields — or regress them — rather than solving per shoe. The report flags this as the reason the cost is acceptable.
06

What it costs you

ConcernThe honest position
precomputeA volumetric mesh and a sparse solve per foot shape. Not per shoe, and not per training step, but it is a real pipeline stage with its own failure modes.
differentiabilityFine with respect to the query point. Differentiating the field with respect to β means differentiating through the solve, which is possible but is work you would rather amortize away.
the outer surfaceA new convention you have to pin down and defend. Change it and every coordinate changes. This is the hidden design decision in option 2.
distortionSmooth and crossing-free is guaranteed. Low distortion is not. Harmonic maps can still stretch badly; they just do it continuously. The section’s own metric — distortion inside material regions, heel block reported separately — is the one to watch.
07

Against option 3

The learned diffeomorphic map buys the same guarantee by a different route. An invertible network is a bijection by architecture, so it also cannot fold. The difference is where the guarantee comes from and what you can say about it.

If you want a read

Option 2 is the one you can defend in a paper without a second experiment. It is deterministic, it is auditable figure by figure, and its guarantee is a citation rather than a training curve. That matters here because ΦF sits underneath everything else — a chart that fails quietly poisons the coverage map, the offsets and the latent space at once, and you would be debugging the decoder for weeks.

Option 3 is the one that most likely wins on the heel-block metric, because it can be told to care about distortion and harmonic interpolation cannot. But you cannot inspect why it wins, and you would be training it against a chart quality metric you have not yet measured.

The report already prescribes the honest order: implement all three, measure non-injective volume fraction and metric distortion on the CAD set with the decoder held fixed, and report the comparison either way. Option 2 is the right thing to build second — after option 1 gives you the baseline numbers that tell you how much the heel block actually costs.
